# Layer 2: Chips and Computers – Listed US and Hong Kong/China Companies Powering AI Compute

## Overview

Layer 2 of Jensen Huang’s “AI industrial cake” consists of the compute engines and supporting hardware that convert electrical power into digital intelligence: GPUs and other accelerators, CPUs, memory, networking silicon, high-bandwidth servers, and advanced cooling systems. This layer is dominated by US-headquartered semiconductor and server OEMs alongside a fast-rising cohort of Chinese AI chipmakers and system vendors, many of which are listing in Hong Kong to access global capital.[^1][^2][^3][^4][^5]

This report focuses on listed US and Hong Kong/China companies that are direct beneficiaries of AI training and inference buildouts – especially those supplying data center GPUs, AI accelerators, high-density AI servers, networking switches for AI clusters, and key Chinese GPU/AI chip names accessible via Hong Kong. It organizes companies into four practical buckets:

- Core AI accelerators and semiconductors (GPUs, AI ASICs, memory).
- AI server OEMs and systems integrators.
- AI data center networking and interconnect vendors.
- Hong Kong/China–listed AI chip and hardware ecosystem.

Each section provides a brief context plus a structured table you can directly extract into spreadsheets or screening tools.

## Core AI Accelerators and Semiconductors (US)

Nvidia remains the global leader in AI accelerators, dominating data center GPU shipments for training and increasingly for inference, with its CUDA software stack underpinning much of the ecosystem. AMD and Intel are the primary US challengers, while Broadcom, Marvell, and others provide custom accelerators, networking ASICs, and supporting silicon used by hyperscalers. Memory vendors such as Micron also benefit from HBM and DDR demand tied to AI servers.[^6][^7][^8][^2][^9]

### Key US-listed AI chip and accelerator names

| Company | Ticker (US) | Role in AI Compute Stack | Notes on AI Exposure |
|---------|-------------|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Nvidia | NVDA (NASDAQ) | Dominant provider of data center GPUs and full-stack AI platform (CUDA, networking, systems) | Market leader in AI training accelerators; full-stack approach gives lowest total cost of ownership for many workloads and underpins much of the current AI infrastructure buildout.[^7][^8][^2] |
| Advanced Micro Devices | AMD (NASDAQ) | Data center GPUs (MI-series), CPUs (EPYC), and adaptive SoCs | Most credible challenger to Nvidia in data center GPUs; MI300 family targets both training and inference with strong hyperscaler adoption.[^10][^2][^9] |
| Intel | INTC (NASDAQ) | CPUs, AI accelerators (Gaudi), and foundry services | Competes in AI inference and training via Gaudi line and Xeon CPUs; also a beneficiary of general server CPU refresh driven by AI.[^2][^9] |

| Micron Technology | MU (NASDAQ) | DRAM and NAND memory including HBM | Highlighted by analysts as a top AI semiconductor pick, driven by surging demand for HBM and high-capacity DRAM in AI servers.[^8][^9] |

| Qualcomm | QCOM (NASDAQ) | AI accelerators for edge, mobile, and some data center use | Expanding AI inference solutions, particularly for edge devices and PCs; smaller direct role in hyperscale training clusters.[^12] |
| Arm Holdings | ARM (NASDAQ) | CPU IP for AI-centric SoCs | Benefits indirectly from shift to custom AI SoCs using Arm CPU cores.[^12] |

## AI Server OEMs and Systems Integrators

High-density AI servers integrate GPUs, CPUs, memory, storage, networking, and advanced cooling into turnkey systems for hyperscalers and enterprises. The AI GPU server system market is dominated by Dell, HPE, Supermicro, and several Asian vendors, with Lenovo as a key Hong Kong–listed player in AI-optimized servers and liquid cooling.[^13][^14][^15]

| Cisco Systems | CSCO (NASDAQ) | Unified compute + networking platforms | Offers AI-optimized UCS servers integrated with its networking stack; more heavily associated with networking but also participates in AI server infrastructure.[^20][^3] |

## AI Data Center Networking and Interconnect Vendors

AI clusters require ultra-high-bandwidth, low-latency networking fabrics, historically dominated by InfiniBand but increasingly shifting to high-performance Ethernet. Key vendors include Nvidia (via Mellanox), Arista Networks, Cisco, and others offering switches, NICs, optics, and full-fabric solutions tailored for AI workloads.[^21][^3][^22][^23]

### Key networking and interconnect names

| Company | Ticker / Listing | Role in AI Networking | Notes on AI Exposure |
|---------|------------------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Nvidia (Networking segment) | NVDA (NASDAQ) | InfiniBand and Ethernet switches, NICs, DPUs | Leads in AI networking ASICs and end-to-end fabrics; core to many of today’s largest AI clusters, tightly integrated with its GPUs.[^3][^23] |
| Arista Networks | ANET (NYSE) | High-performance Ethernet switches for cloud and AI | Leader in data center switching; strong AI infra read-through with 35%+ YoY growth and new AI-focused systems like the 7060XE7 series.[^21][^24][^25] |
| Cisco Systems | CSCO (NASDAQ) | End-to-end networking, optics, and AI fabric solutions | Advancing comprehensive AI networking strategy across systems, silicon, optics, and software; partnering with Nvidia for AI data center networking.[^20][^22][^3] |
| Broadcom (Networking) | AVGO (NASDAQ) | Merchant Ethernet switch and router ASICs | Supplies high-end switch silicon used by cloud providers and OEMs to build AI fabrics.[^6][^3] |
| HPE (Networking) | HPE (NYSE) | AI interconnect (Slingshot) and Ethernet fabrics | Provides advanced interconnect technology for AI and HPC clusters, integrated with its server offerings.[^15][^3] |

China is rapidly building a domestic AI compute stack, including GPUs, AI accelerators, and advanced semiconductors, in response to export controls on foreign GPUs. Many of these firms are either already listed or in the process of listing in Hong Kong, making HKEX a key access point for international investors targeting China’s AI hardware buildout.[^2][^26][^27][^4][^1]

## How to Use This Layer-2 Company Map

The tables above can be directly exported into spreadsheets or watchlists and then segmented by role (accelerator, server OEM, networking, foundry) and geography (US vs Hong Kong/China). For practical portfolio construction, investors can:

- Build a core basket of US AI semiconductor leaders (NVDA, AMD, AVGO, MU, INTC) representing the highest direct sensitivity to AI compute capex.[^7][^8][^9]
- Add beta through AI server OEMs (SMCI, DELL, HPE, Lenovo) that monetize the system-level integration of GPUs, CPUs, memory, and cooling.[^14][^15][^18][^13]
- Layer in AI networking names (ANET, CSCO, Nvidia networking) that benefit from increasing cluster scale and bandwidth requirements.[^3][^23][^21]
- Gain China exposure via Hong Kong–listed hardware names (Lenovo, SMIC, Hua Hong) and upcoming AI chip IPOs (Biren, Kunlunxin, others) that enable domestic AI compute in response to export restrictions.[^26][^27][^1][^4]

A disciplined approach would combine top-down views on AI capex and export-control regimes with bottom-up analysis of each company’s product roadmap, customer mix, and competitive positioning before committing capital.
